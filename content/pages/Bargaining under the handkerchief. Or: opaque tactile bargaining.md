Yesterday we had a reading circle discussion about the book *How Transparency Works*. Rodolfo, a colleague who had read Nethra Samarawickrema’s chapter on the Indian Ocean sapphire trade, mentioned a fascinating example of the transparency/opacity dynamic: bargaining under a handkerchief.[^1]

Briefly, in some commodity markets, bargaining is concealed yet partially transparent. Buyer and seller – or their brokers – clasp hands beneath a handkerchief, sleeve or shawl and communicate prices by touching fingers and knuckles. The cloth makes the exchange legible to the people touching hands while withholding it from everyone around them.

## From Ceylon’s pearl banks to the sapphire trade

Samarawickrema begins with an account published anonymously in volume 61 of the *Journal of the Royal Society of Arts* in 1913 (p. 953). Before deciding whether a pearl fishery would pay, a sample of some 20,000 oysters was lifted and its pearls extracted. “Native Moormen jewellers” then valued them through secret handclasps under a cloth, after sifting and weighing them. The resulting valuation was expressed in old Portuguese and Dutch coinage and, according to the account, had “very little relation” to the pearls’ market value.

The source can be identified more precisely. The journal article was a reprint of a paper Captain John A. Legge read to the Ceylon Natural History Society on 9 October 1912, later published in *Spolia Zeylanica*.[^2] Legge was the Master Attendant of Colombo and Inspector of the Ceylon Pearl Banks, so not an outside observer, but the official who organised and oversaw the valuation. He could describe the administrative procedure in detail, yet the decisive exchange took place in a tactile code under a cloth. In other words, he described a practice he supervised but could not see into.

More than a century later, in 2016, Samarawickrema watched two older Indian gem dealers in Hong Kong demonstrate the same technique. Their families had traded both pearls and sapphires. One finger indicated one, another two, and so on; depending on the stone, a tap could mean a hundred or a thousand. The gestures became invisible when the dealers repeated them beneath a white handkerchief.

The broker did not simply transmit a price. If a seller wanted twenty thousand, he might tap fifty thousand onto the buyer’s hand and say, *Ithukku mele than pohanom* – the price must be upwards of this. He could similarly inflate or deflate what the buyer had offered while bargaining with the seller. Concealment gave the broker room to negotiate with both sides until they reached a price. The two men said that this was how their fathers had brokered deals.

## A mercantile sign language in Indian open-air markets

D. C. Phillott described this practice in Indian open-air markets in 1906.[^3] The negotiating parties hid their right hands under a handkerchief, coat or turban and used combinations of fingers to indicate units, tens, hundreds or thousands. Jewellers and cloth merchants had variations of the code. It was not a substitute for a shared spoken language, but a way of preventing bystanders from learning the price.

More than a century later, a study of Chhawani Mandi in Indore found trader-to-trader deals still being made, literally and figuratively, “under the handkerchief” or “under the sleeve”.[^4] The practice belongs to a market organisation in which brokers, commission agents and privileged information remain important.

## Somaliland: brokers bargaining with brokers

Ahmed M. Musa and Raphael Schwere provide the richest ethnography of the practice.[^5] In Somaliland’s livestock markets, a seller normally appoints one broker and a buyer another; the buyer and seller may never meet. The two brokers grasp right hands, cover them with a shawl or turban, and encode offers digit by digit through finger grips, knuckle pressure and bent fingertips. They may argue aloud about an animal’s qualities while keeping the only sensitive information – the price – tactile and hidden.

If they cannot agree, a third broker, the *isku soo jiide*, may intervene. He first learns the buyer broker’s offer under the cloth and then bargains separately, again under the cloth, with the seller broker. This is therefore a three-person mediation conducted through successive two-person exchanges, not necessarily three people negotiating with their hands beneath one handkerchief at once.

Concealment keeps rival bidders and curious onlookers from intervening. It can speed up deals, permit different prices for similar animals and protect the brokers’ specialist role. The same opacity disadvantages newcomers and livestock owners who cannot judge the prevailing range, which helps explain longstanding accusations that brokers use the cloth to cheat.

## What does the cloth do?

“Silent bargaining” is slightly misleading. These negotiations can be noisy, argumentative and socially dense. Only the numerical offer is removed from public view. The practice is better understood as **opaque tactile bargaining**: not an absence of communication, but a careful distribution of transparency.

The handkerchief creates a temporary enclosure around price information. It lets selected insiders communicate with precision while excluding competitors and spectators. It can also place buyers and sellers in an asymmetric relation to the broker, who knows what both sides have said while controlling what each learns about the other.

Legge’s account makes the point particularly clearly. The covered exchange was not beyond colonial administration; it was folded into a valuation procedure run by the Inspector of the Pearl Banks. The surrounding process was documented, measured and supervised, while its crucial moment remained illegible to the supervising official.
